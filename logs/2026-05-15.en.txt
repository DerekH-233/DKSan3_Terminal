At 03:17 AM, the deep-space station in the Craigieburn Mountains, New Zealand, received the final reading. Comet R3 was sliding past Orion's Belt, its ion tail unfurling across wide-field images as silver-blue filaments akin to a nebula. This was no ordinary transit; the pervasive ion flow interwove with the glow of the Orion Nebula (M42), and the dark red traces of Barnard's Loop pierced through the coma, creating a rare double superposition effect. Historical references show that the Great Comet of 1264 and Comet Hale-Bopp in 1997 lingered at the same position, but R3's particle trails were longer with higher inter-band dispersion—suggesting its nucleus is accelerating in disintegration.
The Joint Science Department requests declassification of carbon-based molecular spectral lines in the comet's tail, but strong background light has degraded the signal-to-noise ratio, necessitating a restart of the cooling system. The attrition is acceptable. R3 is receding, yet I still gaze at the screen: Orion's double stars flicker amid the dust, a star's death and a comet's demise exchanging negligible mass in the same deep space. Recording complete.
